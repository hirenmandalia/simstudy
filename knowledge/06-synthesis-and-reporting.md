# Synthesis and reporting

## Process
1. **Re-read the research questions.** Synthesis exists to answer them.
2. **Extract observations per participant:** task outcomes, problems (with the screen they happened on), expectations, reactions and notable quotes. This is *data*: raw observations that haven't been analysed yet [1].
3. **Cluster observations into themes.** Group related observations, label the groups, then prioritise them. This is affinity diagramming [2], or thematic analysis: code the data, form candidate themes, then review them [3][4]. Record how many participants each theme involves and which personas.
   - **Use constant comparison, so nothing is cherry-picked.** Go question by question. For each comment, ask whether it answers this question. Then ask whether it says the same thing as an existing theme; if not, start a new one. Repeat until every comment is placed. Split themes that are too broad and merge overlapping ones [11].
   - Considering *every* participant's comments stops the analyst from keeping only the striking quotes. Keep a short note of why things were grouped the way they were [11].
4. **Keep the levels of analysis separate** [1]:
   - **Finding (observation):** a pattern across the data. *What happened.*
   - **Insight (interpretation):** why it happened and what it means, drawing on context. *Labelled as interpretation.*
   - **Recommendation:** what to do, based on the insight.
5. **Look for contradictions and minority views.** Explain them by differences between personas where possible, rather than averaging them away. A single unusual comment can be the most important finding, for example an issue only one person is positioned to see. Examine outliers rather than discarding them, and say why they matter [11].
6. **Weigh evidence by more than count** [11][12]:
   - **Extensiveness:** how many *different* participants raised it.
   - **Frequency:** how often it came up.
   - **Specificity:** concrete, first-hand detail outweighs general statements.
   - **Intensity:** strength of feeling. For simulated personas, intensity is generated rather than felt, so give it little weight (07).
   - Frequency alone is not importance [11].
7. **Rate severity** (see 02) and include positive findings [5].
8. **Answer each research question directly,** with a confidence level and evidence.
9. **Write recommendations that follow from findings.** Make them specific (e.g. "rename the button", not "redesign the page") and link each one to the finding it addresses [5]. Keep design changes separate from further research.

## Numbers from small samples
- Qualitative studies show *what* problems exist and *why*. They don't show *how many* people would hit them [6].
- Small samples have large measurement error. For example, a 50% success rate from 10 users is consistent with a true rate of roughly 24–76% [7].
- Report counts ("4 of 6 personas"), not percentages, and never present simulated counts as real-world rates.

## Quotes
- Quotes are persuasive evidence when they are chosen with care [8].
- Attribute each quote to an anonymised participant rather than a name [9]. In this product that means the persona name, labelled "AI persona", plus the transcript line id.
- Use verbatim excerpts only, never paraphrased text in quotation marks. Choose quotes that illustrate a finding, including negative ones.

## Report structure for decision-makers
Write in layers [10]:
- **Headlines** for executives and busy readers: the 3–5 things they must know.
- **Themes and answers to the research questions** for the product manager.
- **Detail and method** for specialists: scope, participants, task results, evidence and limitations.

In this product the report contains:
- Title and scope: feature, decision, method, participants and screens.
- A headline summary.
- An answer to each research question.
- Task results.
- Findings with severity, frequency and evidence.
- Patterns and contradictions.
- Prioritised recommendations linked to findings.
- Limitations and what to validate with real users.
- A statement that the decision belongs to the PM and team.

## Quality bar
- **Systematic and verifiable:** the steps from conversation to report are defined, could be explained to a reviewer, and leave an evidence trail (transcript line ids, verified quotes) that lets anyone retrace a finding. Reviewers' core worry is that the analyst picked the ideas they liked [11].
- **Rigour matches the stakes:** fast, notes-level analysis suits low-stakes, reversible decisions. Bigger decisions need fuller analysis [11] and, for simulated research, real-user validation (07).
- Every claim is traceable to transcript evidence.
- Confidence is stated honestly; "low" is acceptable and useful.
- No overclaiming: simulated, small-sample evidence is directional (see 07).
