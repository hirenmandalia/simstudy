# Lessons on analysing focus groups (University of Minnesota teaching session)

> **Source:** UMN_MCH (University of Minnesota, Maternal & Child Health). (2019, 15 Jul). *Focus Group Session 4: Analysis* [Video, 41 min]. YouTube. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5sMe_iQfd0s
> A recorded teaching session by two instructors, consistent with Richard Krueger and Mary Anne Casey (authors of the focus-group method cited in 03). The speakers aren't named on the page; we infer this from the content and from the related Krueger video.
> These notes summarise the session in our own words, from its auto-generated captions; timestamps are approximate. No transcript is reproduced.
> It is a quality reference for *how to analyse and report*, not evidence about any product.

## Two principles: systematic and verifiable [~01:20–04:30]
- **Systematic:** follow a defined sequence of steps for capturing, documenting and coding data, as you would in quantitative analysis. You must also be able to *explain* the process to others. Reviewers' real worry is that the analyst simply picked the ideas they liked.
- **Verifiable:** leave a trail of evidence so each step from conversation to report could be retraced: recording, transcript or notes, codes, then report.
- **Analysis depends on earlier steps.** Inconsistent questions across groups, or moderators who didn't follow up, can make analysis nearly impossible. Keep the questions consistent so groups can be compared [~00:40].

## Match the rigour to the stakes [~04:40–05:20, ~12:00–13:20]
- How thorough the analysis should be depends on how big and reversible the decision is, and on the standard expected: a dissertation, a journal article, a community newsletter.
- **Full transcripts** suit high-stakes studies. **Notes-based analysis** can be enough for fast, low-stakes work such as piloting options, but carries more risk of overlooking something.

## Analysis starts during the group [~05:20–06:40]
- While moderating, keep asking yourself: is this concrete enough, and do I need an explanation? Push for detail that will be usable in analysis.
- Understanding is **emergent:** it grows with each group until **saturation**, when new groups add little.
- A **final question** in each group, such as "of everything discussed, what is most important?" or "what advice would you give the people designing this?", gives analysis a direct read on priorities [~08:40].

## Capture the right data [~06:40–08:40]
- Brief the note-taker on what to capture. Meeting-style minutes are useless for analysis.
- **Quotes are the evidence** that supports the summary points in a report.
- Keep everything participants produce: lists, drawings, whiteboard photos.

## Debrief immediately after each group [~09:20–11:20]
- Before leaving, the moderator and note-taker check the recording, then spend about 15–20 minutes discussing:
  - the most important ideas;
  - standout quotes;
  - how this group differed from others;
  - surprises;
  - anything to change in recruiting or the questions.
- Memory fades fast, so this is a "memory dump" into a permanent record. The debrief can double as a quick group summary.

## Coding: constant comparison, not cherry-picking [~14:00–19:20]
- Analysis means sorting comments into categories, like sorting laundry.
- The **constant comparative** method (attributed to Glaser and Strauss), question by question:
  1. Take each quote and ask whether it answers this question. If it answers another question, move it there. If it answers none, set it aside.
  2. If it does answer the question, ask whether it says the same thing as an existing pile. If yes, add it; if not, start a new pile.
  3. Repeat for every quote, then for each transcript in turn.
  4. Split piles that are too broad, merge related ones, and give each a label.
- Because *every* piece of data is examined, nobody can pick out only the comments they like.
- **Write memos** recording why things went into each category, so coding stays consistent.
- Doing this with a second person, ideally someone who was in the groups, helps.
- **Tools:** paper and flip charts, Word or Excel, or qualitative-analysis software. Software stores and retrieves codes but doesn't tell you what the data means [~19:20–21:20].

## Don't let counting decide what matters [~21:20–28:30]
- **Frequency** (how often something is said) and **extensiveness** (how many *different* people said it) are both useful. Neither automatically equals importance.
- **Outliers can be the most important finding.** A single comment may come from the one person who can see an emerging issue. Examine fringe views rather than discarding them, and report them when the context suggests they matter.
- Percentages in focus-group reports are suspect, because not everyone answers every question.
- **Weigh intensity:** strength of feeling, such as raised voices, everyone talking at once, or tears. Emotion is often closely tied to behaviour.
  - In one example, a small group's intense feelings about a local service change led the instructors to advise the client to back away, even though most residents didn't care [~28:40–30:40].
- **Weigh specificity:** detailed, first-hand accounts of a vivid experience deserve more weight than general statements.
- **Get feelings into words.** Body language isn't in a transcript, and interpreting it can be wrong. When people nod or gesture, ask them to say what they mean ("I see some head nods; would someone comment on that?") so there is a quote [~26:30, ~39:20].
- An instructor suggested, but hadn't tested, asking for a 1–10 intensity rating when strong emotion appears, to show readers how strong a feeling was [~37:30].
- Whoever does the analysis should attend the groups if possible. Intensity is lost in a transcript.

## Close the loop with participants [~32:40–36:45]
- Send participants a thank-you note or report covering what was learned, what will happen, what can't be done and why, and a contact for questions.
- Repeated research with no visible follow-up erodes trust, especially in communities that get studied often. Commit to ongoing follow-up, not a one-off.
- This applies to real research only, not to simulated participants.

## What this means for SimStudy's synthesis
| Lesson | How it applies |
|---|---|
| Systematic and verifiable | Already partly built: transcript line ids, verified quotes and versioned artifacts form an audit trail. The report should say briefly *how* findings were derived. |
| Constant comparison, no cherry-picking | Synthesis should consider every persona's lines for each research question, not just striking quotes. Weak or contrary evidence must appear too. |
| Frequency and extensiveness aren't importance | Report "how many personas", but rank findings by severity, specificity and intensity as well as count. |
| Keep outliers | A single persona's unique concern can be a finding. Say why it matters rather than dropping it. |
| Weigh specificity and intensity | Give more weight to concrete, step-level reactions (e.g. "I tapped Save and nothing told me where it went") than to generic praise or complaints. Simulated "intensity" is generated, not felt, so treat it cautiously (07). |
| Get feelings into words | Moderator probes should ask personas to put a reaction into words rather than infer it. |
| Debrief after each session | Possible enhancement: a short automatic debrief after each run (key ideas, surprises, what to change in the guide) for the PM. |
| Match rigour to stakes | Simulated research suits fast, low-stakes, reversible decisions. Higher-stakes decisions need real-user validation (07). |
| Close the loop | Not applicable to simulated participants. |
