# Choosing a research method

## Start from the research goals and questions
A research plan names the research questions or goals before choosing a method [1]. In this product, also name the decision the study informs (build or launch) and what evidence would change the PM's mind. This framing is our own guidance, not taken from a source.

Qualitative methods answer "why" and "how to fix" questions. Quantitative methods answer "how many" and "how much" questions [2]. A small qualitative study, real or simulated, can't forecast business outcomes such as revenue or adoption rates. Reframe those as questions about understanding, behaviour or perceived value.

## Usability test (behavioural, task-based)
Usability testing means watching people attempt realistic tasks so you can find problems in a design [3]. It is a behavioural method [2]. Use it when the research questions ask whether people *can* use something: finding an entry point, understanding labels and flows, where they get stuck, and mismatches between what they expect and what the UI does.
- It needs something to react to: screens, a prototype or a built feature, plus realistic tasks.
- It suits "launch" decisions on partially built features and checking a flow before engineering invests in it.

## Focus group (attitudinal, group discussion)
Focus groups are an attitudinal method [2]. They are useful for exploring what people want, how they feel about a concept, and the words they use. They are a poor way to evaluate the usability of an interface, because watching or discussing a design is fundamentally different from using it [4]. Use a focus group for perceptions, desirability, objections and comparisons with alternatives. It suits "build" decisions on early ideas.

## When the choice is unclear
- If the questions mix "can they use it?" and "do they want it?", recommend a usability test with a debrief interview about value, or two separate studies. Explain the trade-off and let the PM choose.
- If there are no screens and the question is about usability, a usability study can't run. Ask for screens or reframe the study around reactions to the concept.

## Number of participants
- **Finding problems in qualitative usability testing:** about 5 users uncover most problems that affect a large share of users. Nielsen recommends several small rounds rather than one big study [5][6]. With several distinct user groups, use about 3–4 users per group [5].
- **Caveats:**
  - The 5-user rule holds only for problems that affect roughly a third or more of users. Rarer problems need larger samples [7].
  - The rule is not suitable for comparing designs or estimating completion rates [7].
  - Random sets of 5 users found anywhere from 55% to 99% of the known problems in one study [8].
  - Quantitative studies need about 20 users [5].
- **Focus groups:** about 6–9 participants per group, and run more than one group [4].
- **This product** caps simulated studies at **12** participants. Adding simulated participants does not add real-world statistical validity, so diverse persona characteristics matter more than headcount (see 07).

## Session length (nominal, for planning)
- **Usability test:** typically 60–90 minutes; participants tire after that [9]. Shorter sessions of about 30 minutes are common for a few tasks [10].
- **Focus group:** about 2 hours [4].
