# Focus groups

## What they're for (and not for)
Focus groups explore attitudes, perceptions, desires and the language people use. They are prone to groupthink: strong personalities skew what gets shared [1]. They capture what people *say*, which often differs from what they *do*. They are a poor method for judging interface usability, because watching or discussing a design is not the same as using it [2]. For detailed usability findings, run a usability test instead [1].

## Discussion guide (questioning route)
A widely taught sequence, credited to Krueger's focus-group method, uses five question types [3][4]:
1. **Opening:** a quick, easy, factual question everyone answers in turn, to get people talking [4][7].
2. **Introductory:** introduces the topic, e.g. current habits or the last time they did something.
3. **Transition:** moves toward the key topic, e.g. frustrations with how they do it today.
4. **Key questions (2–5):** the core questions that answer the research questions. Most of the time goes here [4]. Show the concept or screens at this point.
5. **Ending:** a round asking what was the most important thing said, then a short summary that participants confirm or correct, and "Is there anything we missed?" [4][7].

Our sources for the five names are secondary. Check them against Krueger & Casey [3] before citing them as Krueger's.

## Moderation
- **Manage dominant talkers.** Interrupt politely and ask for a different viewpoint [5][2]. Acknowledge briefly, then pass the floor to someone else by name [7].
- **Draw in quieter participants.** Invite them by name, use warm-ups, and offer written as well as spoken ways to contribute [5][1].
- **Seek disagreement.** Ask follow-ups in both positive and negative forms, e.g. "Does anyone see it differently?" Contradictions are data, not noise [1].
- **Probe for specifics.** "Would you give me an example of what you mean?" [5] Probe any cryptic or vague remark [7].
- **Encourage cross-talk.** Participants responding to each other, not just to the moderator, is a sign the group is working [7].
- **Turn quick ratings into discussion.** Show of fingers, thumbs or short rating sheets only start the conversation. Always ask why [7].
- **Stay neutral.** Don't reveal your opinion or the team's hopes (see 04).
- **Group size:** about 6–9 participants, ideally across more than one group [2].

## Analysis
Krueger's systematic analysis process [6]:
- Look for themes question by question, then across the whole discussion.
- Weigh how many participants raised a point (extensiveness), and how often it came up (frequency).
- Weigh strength of feeling (intensity).
- Weigh specificity: first-hand, specific accounts count more than hypotheticals.
- Note what was *not* said, and why opinions shifted during the discussion.
- Report how widely a view was shared, e.g. "4 of 6 participants".

Minority views that may matter for a particular segment are worth reporting separately; this is our own guidance, implied by [6]. Never treat stated intent from a focus group as a forecast of usage [2].
