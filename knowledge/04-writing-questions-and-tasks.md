# Writing neutral questions, probes and scripts

## Principles
- **Open, not closed.** Open questions surface unexpected insights. Start with "how", "what" or "Tell me about…" rather than "did" or "was". Closed questions are for clarifying facts [1].
  - Closed: "Do you like the collections feature?"
  - Open: "Tell me how you keep track of recipes you want to make."
- **Neutral, not leading.** A leading question contains or implies the answer you want. Common traps [2]:
  - rephrasing what the participant said in your own words;
  - naming interface elements;
  - assuming how they feel.
  - Leading: "How easy was it to find the filter?" Neutral: "Tell me about finding a recipe that fits your guest."
- **One question at a time.** Split double-barrelled questions [3], and avoid compound questions that overload working memory [4].
- **Specific past instances over hypotheticals.** People are poor predictors of their own future behaviour, so avoid "Would you ever…?" [4][5]. Ask about specific recent instances instead, e.g. "Tell me about the last time you…" [6]. Memories are imperfect too, so prefer observing behaviour (a usability task) where possible [5].
- **Don't sell.** Never describe benefits before asking for reactions; doing so primes participants [2][7].

## Useful neutral probes
- "Tell me more about that." [1]
- "Would you give me an example of what you mean?" (from the focus-group moderation guidance in 03)
- **Echo:** repeat the participant's last word or phrase as a question, e.g. "…confusing?" [8]
- **Boomerang:** hand their question back neutrally, e.g. "What do you think it does?" [8]
- **Columbo:** a short, trailing, unfinished question that invites them to continue, e.g. "And this button is…?" [8]
- "What did you expect to happen?" / "What were you looking for at that point?"
- Pause before probing, and speak only when needed [8].

## Moderator script checklist
- The introduction states the purpose of the session and that there are no right or wrong answers [6]. It makes clear that the design is being tested, not the participant [9].
- Don't reveal hypotheses or the team's hopes. Wording and facilitator behaviour can prime participants [7][2].
- Ask participants to think aloud (usability) or to respond to each other (focus group).
- Warm-up questions relate to the research questions.
- Every task and question maps to a research question; drop anything that doesn't.
- The close thanks participants and invites final thoughts.

## Red flags to fix before approval
- A task names an on-screen label or describes the UI path (see 02).
- A question presumes a feeling, e.g. "What did you love about…" [2].
- A hypothetical "Would you…" question where a past-instance question would work [4].
- A research question with no task or question covering it.
- Too many tasks or questions for the session length (see 01).
